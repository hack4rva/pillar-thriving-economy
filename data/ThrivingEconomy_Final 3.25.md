**THRIVING ECONOMY\
Workshop Discussion Themes: Targeted Problem Statements**

**1. The MBE goal-setting problem feels technically valid, but not exciting enough on its own for a hackathon**

Participants generally agreed that the City's challenge around setting more precise, project-specific MBE goals is real and important, but the first targeted version felt too technical and too close to work the City already partially does through the disparity study. The group questioned whether a hackathon team would find the problem compelling enough and whether the solution would generate meaningfully new value given that much of the baseline data already exists.

**2. The bigger opportunity is not only setting better MBE targets, but strengthening the pipeline of minority-owned firms and workers**

The discussion repeatedly moved away from narrow goal-setting and toward the broader structural issue: there are not enough minority-owned firms in certain trades, and the pipeline into those trades is weak. Participants highlighted outdated technical education, limited teacher capacity, lack of student awareness, and broader barriers to entering and staying in construction-related fields. This shifted energy away from compliance metrics and toward capacity-building, access, and long-term wealth creation.

**3. Small business navigation is a stronger and more hackable problem**

Compared with the MBE goal-setting work, the small business navigation problem felt more immediate, easier to understand, and more likely to attract a hackathon team. Participants agreed that emerging business owners struggle to understand where to start, what steps apply to them, and which City office to contact first. This makes the experience confusing for business owners and inefficient for staff.

**4. The real navigation challenge is both front-end and back-end**

The conversation revealed two layers to the small business problem. On the front end, entrepreneurs need a simpler, more customer-friendly pathway to understand licenses, permits, and services. On the back end, the City's departments operate across disconnected systems that do not share information, forcing business owners to re-enter the same information repeatedly. Participants saw this lack of system connectivity as a much bigger transformation opportunity than simply improving website content.

**5. Business registration, compliance, and visibility are deeply connected**

The group noted that many businesses may not realize they need local registration or may delay it intentionally because the process is confusing or feels punitive. That means the City lacks good visibility into how many businesses exist, which ones are compliant, and how to connect them to services or opportunity. Participants saw a connection between better onboarding, better data, and better business support.

**6. The City has useful data, but it is scattered and hard to use**

Participants emphasized that the City and its partners already hold valuable data across multiple systems, studies, and platforms. The problem is less about lack of information and more about poor access, poor integration, and limited ability to turn that information into something actionable for staff, entrepreneurs, or partners.

**Workshop Discussion Themes: Blue Sky Discussion**

**1. There is a broader opportunity to connect City systems and make data usable across many functions**

The strongest Blue Sky energy in the room centered on the idea that Richmond's internal systems are too fragmented. Participants described a much bigger opportunity to create a hub or connective layer that allows multiple City systems to share data, making it easier to support not just small businesses, but many other City services. This was framed as potentially transformational, even if too large for a narrow targeted challenge.

**2. Generative technology could help analyze City data and proactively surface opportunities**

Participants were interested in using AI or generative technology to analyze the City's various databases, processes, and studies in ways that proactively push relevant opportunities to people. Instead of requiring someone to already know what to look for, a future system could help surface permits, registrations, contracting opportunities, workforce gaps, or support resources based on context and need.

**3. There is a meaningful opportunity to improve how minority-owned businesses get discovered and connected to contracts**

Although the narrower MBE targeted problem lost momentum, the Blue Sky discussion surfaced a more compelling angle: improving the tools that help minority-owned firms get on the relevant lists, get found, and get matched to real contract opportunities. Participants discussed both discoverability and job/opportunity matching as potential opportunity areas.

**4. The construction and trade pipeline challenge is broader than contracting alone**

The workshop surfaced a larger ecosystem issue around trades and workforce development: outdated technical school infrastructure, teacher shortages, weak marketing of trade pathways, and disconnects between local jobs and local talent. Participants suggested there may be a compelling Blue Sky question around how technology could help people understand, prepare for, and enter these pathways more effectively---especially with an explicit minority wealth-building lens.

**5. Richmond needs a more strategic way to connect business development with actual economic need**

Participants also raised a broader economic development question: how can the City better understand where there are real market gaps, job opportunities, skill shortages, and business needs---and then use that to guide entrepreneurs, support organizations, and workforce partners more intelligently?

**PILLAR: THRIVING ECONOMY\
Revised Targeted Problem Statement: Helping Minority-Owned Businesses Identify Relevant City Contract Opportunities**

**Problem Statement**\
How might we use technology to help minority-owned businesses in Richmond more easily identify and understand City contracting opportunities that match their capabilities, so that more firms can confidently explore and pursue public work?

**Context**\
The City of Richmond publishes procurement opportunities through its vendor portal: <https://mvendor.cgieva.com/Vendor/public/AllOpportunities.jsp?&agencyname=City%20of%20Richmond%20Government>

While the portal provides transparent access to contracting opportunities, it is designed primarily for vendors already familiar with government procurement processes. Many small or first-time contractors---including minority-owned businesses---may struggle to determine which opportunities are relevant to their services, interpret procurement terminology, or understand what the first step toward participation might be. As a result, businesses that could potentially participate in City projects may overlook opportunities or assume they are not eligible. Improving how businesses interpret and navigate existing procurement listings could help more firms discover relevant opportunities and take the first step toward participating in City contracting.

**Constraints**

- Existing procurement policies and legal requirements cannot be changed

- The existing procurement portal must remain the official source for opportunity postings

- The solution should rely on publicly available procurement data or information

- The tool should be accessible to small businesses with little or no prior government contracting experience

**Success Would Mean**

- Businesses can more quickly determine whether an opportunity matches their services

- Procurement listings are easier for first-time vendors to interpret and understand

- More minority-owned businesses feel confident exploring City contracting opportunities

- Businesses are better prepared before contacting City procurement staff

**Example Ideas a Hackathon Group Might Create**

**Opportunity Match Tool:** A tool that allows businesses to enter their services, certifications, or capabilities and receive a curated list of relevant opportunities pulled from the City procurement portal.

**Plain-Language Opportunity Explorer:** A searchable interface that summarizes procurement listings in simple language, explaining the type of work involved, likely contractor roles, and key next steps.

**MBE Opportunity Alerts:** A notification system that allows businesses to subscribe to alerts when new opportunities related to their services appear in the procurement portal.

**PILLAR: THRIVING ECONOMY\
Revised Targeted Problem Statement: Small Business Navigation & Business Setup Friction**

**Problem Statement\**
How might we use technology to improve how emerging and home-based entrepreneurs start and register a business in Richmond so that they can understand the right steps, complete them in the right order, and connect to relevant City services without confusion or repeated handoffs, while respecting existing departmental structures, program eligibility rules, and staffing capacity?

**Context**\
First-time and small-scale entrepreneurs in Richmond often need to interact with multiple City departments---including licensing, Minority Business, Economic Development, code enforcement, and workforce-related services---to start and operate legally. However, there is no clear front door, no coordinated pathway, and no simple way to understand which requirements apply, what order steps should happen in, or where to go for help. Many business owners are unsure whether they need a business license, whether a home-based business is allowed, or what registrations and permits apply to them. As a result, they make avoidable mistakes, delay compliance, or abandon the process altogether, while City staff spend time redirecting inquiries across disconnected systems.

**Constraints**

- Existing departmental roles and structures remain in place

- Program eligibility rules must remain intact

- No creation of a new large concierge office

- Data-sharing between departments is limited

- Licensing and regulatory policies cannot be altered

**Success would mean**

- Entrepreneurs can quickly understand what applies to them

- Required steps are completed in the correct sequence

- Fewer businesses unintentionally operate out of compliance

- Staff spend less time redirecting inquiries

- Business owners have a clearer, more customer-friendly starting experience

**Example ideas a hackathon group might create\**

**Start Here Business Wizard:** A guided intake flow that asks plain-language questions and generates a personalized checklist of licenses, permits, offices, and next steps in the right sequence.

**Business Setup Copilot:** A City-specific AI assistant trained on Richmond's business requirements that answers common setup questions, explains which offices do what, and links directly to forms and contacts.

**Small Business Pathway Dashboard:** A tool that shows business owners where they are in the setup process, what they've completed, what is next, and what City services or support programs they may be eligible for.

PILLAR: THRIVING ECONOMY\
OLD Version 2: Business Compliance, Visibility & Lost Revenue**

**Problem Statement**

How might we use tech to improve how small and home-based businesses understand and complete required local registration and licensing steps so that more businesses operate in compliance and the City has clearer visibility into business activity, while respecting legal limits on data sharing and enforcement authority?

**Context**

Many entrepreneurs register an LLC at the state level but do not complete required local licensing steps, such as obtaining a business license (including BPOL) or confirming whether their home-based business complies with zoning rules. This gap is often unintentional and driven by:

- Confusion about local requirements

- Unclear communication about next steps

- Fragmented information across departments

At the same time, City departments collect related business data separately and cannot easily share or synthesize it. The City cannot confidently answer:

- How many businesses are operating

- How many are properly licensed

- Where compliance gaps exist

This creates inconsistent enforcement, lost revenue, and missed opportunities for proactive outreach and support.

**Constraints**

- Legal and policy limits on sharing certain business data

- Existing licensing, regulatory, and procurement rules remain in place

- Staffing capacity is fixed; no large new enforcement unit

- Budget constraints require practical, prototype-ready solutions

- Solutions should not rely primarily on punitive enforcement

**Success would mean**

- More businesses complete required local registration steps

- Entrepreneurs better understand compliance requirements early

- The City has improved visibility into business activity trends

- Revenue leakage from unregistered businesses decreases

- Outreach is proactive and supportive rather than enforcement-first

**EXAMPLE IDEAS A HACKATHON GROUP MIGHT GENERATE**

**Business Compliance Self-Check Tool**

A non-punitive, self-service compliance scan that's designed as supportive, not enforcement-first.

- "Have you filed X?", "Are you operating from home?", "Do you employ staff?"

Outputs:

- Risk flags, Required next steps, Contact info for help

**Business Lifecycle Intelligence Dashboard (Internal)**

Pulls together:

- State LLC filings (public), Local licensing records, Short-term rental registrations, Code enforcement signals

Shows:

- Potential unlicensed businesses, Registration gaps, Trends by geography
